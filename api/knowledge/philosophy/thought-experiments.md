---
title: Thought experiments are not evidence
tag: philosophy
source: cognite-original
---

A thought experiment is a carefully designed imaginary case: a runaway trolley, a brain in a vat, a violinist plugged into your circulatory system overnight. Philosophers use them to isolate a principle. If you say "always minimize deaths," the trolley case asks whether you would still say that when minimizing deaths means diverting a train yourself.

The method is powerful and abused. It is powerful because real life arrives messy, with extra facts that let you dodge the principle. It is abused when the story is so science-fictional that your intuition is just a reaction to weirdness. "I would not push the large person" might be a moral judgment, a disgust response, or a refusal to accept the premise.

Good use of thought experiments treats them as questions, not as data. They reveal what you are already committed to. They do not replace history, psychology, or the details of an actual hospital. When a public debate is conducted only in hypotheticals, ask what real situation is being avoided.

You can steal the tool without the jargon. Before you adopt a rule ("I never skip," "I always optimize"), invent a case where the rule looks cruel or silly. If you still want the rule, keep it. If not, you have learned something cheaply, without waiting for the world to stage the example.
