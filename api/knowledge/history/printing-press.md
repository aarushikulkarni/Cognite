---
title: The printing press as a network effect
tag: history
source: cognite-original
---

Johannes Gutenberg's press, developed in Mainz in the mid-1400s, combined movable metal type, oil-based ink, and a screw press. Printing itself was not new: China and Korea had earlier woodblock and metal-type traditions. What changed in Europe was speed of spread plus a market of vernacular readers, universities, and churches that wanted identical copies.

A book copied by hand is a performance. A printed book is a small factory run. Once type is set, the cost of the next copy drops. That makes pamphlets, indulgences, scientific tables, and news sheets economically possible. It also makes censorship a different problem: you can burn a manuscript; you cannot easily burn a whole edition that has already ridden out of town.

Historians still argue about how much the press "caused" the Reformation, the Scientific Revolution, or nationalism. A cautious claim is enough: cheaper identical texts made it easier to compare claims, quote chapter and verse, and notice contradictions. Maps, music, and mathematical notation benefited from the same trick.

The modern echo is not "screens are the new press" as a slogan. It is that communication technology changes who can publish, how fast error travels, and what counts as a shared reference. Movable type did not make people wise. It changed the cost of making an argument public.
