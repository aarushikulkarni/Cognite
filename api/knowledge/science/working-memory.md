---
title: Working memory has a small stage
tag: science
source: cognite-original
---

Working memory is the small amount of information you can hold in mind and manipulate at once: a phone number you are about to dial, the beginning of a sentence you are still writing, the rule you are using to sort objects. It is not the same as long-term memory, which can store languages, faces, and routes for years.

Laboratory tasks suggest the stage is limited. Classic estimates talked about "seven plus or minus two" chunks. Later work often finds fewer, especially if the items are novel and you cannot group them. Chunking is the trick: "C-I-A" is three letters or one agency. Expertise is, in part, better chunking.

Attention and working memory are close neighbors. Distraction does not always erase knowledge; it can simply knock the current items off the stage. That is one reason a notification during a proof, a recipe, or a delicate conversation feels costly. You may still "know" the material, but you have to reload it.

None of this requires claiming that an app can see your networks fire. The practical implication is architectural. If a task needs several pieces held together — an argument, a budget, a design constraint — protect a few quiet minutes. Externalize what you can onto paper. Build one chunk at a time. The brain's stage is small. Good work respects the furniture.
