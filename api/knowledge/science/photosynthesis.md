---
title: Photosynthesis in one tight story
tag: science
source: cognite-original
---

Photosynthesis is how most of the living world captures sunlight and stores it as chemical energy. In plants, algae, and many bacteria, pigments such as chlorophyll absorb photons. That energy is used to split water, release oxygen, and build energy carriers. Those carriers then help stitch carbon dioxide into sugars.

The story has two linked acts. The light-dependent reactions happen in membranes and produce ATP, NADPH, and oxygen. The Calvin cycle, which does not need photons at every step but needs the products of the first act, fixes carbon. Glucose and other carbohydrates are later used for growth, storage, or respiration.

This is why plants matter far beyond gardens. They are an interface between the sun and the food web. The oxygen in the air you are breathing is a waste product of water-splitting that accumulated over geological time. Fossil fuels are, roughly, ancient photosynthesis packed into a dense, slow-to-renew form.

A common confusion is to treat plants as "making energy from nothing." They do not. They convert one form of energy into another, with losses, like every other physical process. The elegance is the conversion, not a violation of thermodynamics. When you eat a fruit, you are borrowing sunlight that was captured on a leaf.
