---
title: Feedback loops in ordinary life
tag: science
source: cognite-original
---

A feedback loop happens when a system's output becomes part of its later input. A thermostat is the classroom example: the heater warms the room, a sensor reads the temperature, and the heater shuts off. That is negative feedback, which stabilizes. A microphone too close to a speaker is positive feedback: sound in, louder sound out, until the room rings.

Biology is dense with both. Insulin helps bring high blood sugar down. Hunger and satiety hormones push eating up or down. Predator and prey populations can oscillate. Climate systems include loops too: melting ice can expose darker water that absorbs more heat, which melts more ice.

The scientific habit is to ask which loop you are in before you "fix" a number. Pushing on a stabilizing loop can look like nothing is happening, until it suddenly is. Pushing on a reinforcing loop can look like rapid success, until it overshoots.

You do not need laboratory equipment to use the idea. Sleep, caffeine, scrolling, and mood can form loops. A late night makes the next day harder, which makes another late night tempting. Naming the loop does not automatically break it, but it gives you a place to intervene: earlier bedtime, a walk, a different first action in the morning. Science, here, is a way of seeing circular causes instead of a single villain.
